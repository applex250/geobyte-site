Geobyte: Geology × Computing Lab

About Geobyte

Geobyte is an interdisciplinary laboratory where geologists and computer scientists build new ways to read the Earth.

We combine field observations, drill cores, satellite imagery, geochemistry, and seismic signals with machine learning, spatial computing, and high-performance simulation. The goal is not to replace geological judgement, but to make more evidence legible, comparable, and useful.

Our team works across the full path from an outcrop to a reproducible model: collect, encode, interpret, simulate, and share.

We design systems that remain transparent about uncertainty, preserve the context of every measurement, and help researchers ask better questions of complex Earth data.

Computational fieldwork

Geobyte connects fieldwork to computation: a rock sample, a satellite pixel, or a seismic trace becomes a structured observation that can be queried, compared, and modeled.

Our tools reduce repetitive preparation while keeping experts in the loop. Every prediction carries provenance, confidence, and a path back to the evidence that produced it.

The same workflow can support mineral exploration, basin analysis, tectonic mapping, and geohazard assessment without flattening their geological differences.

We build for real research environments: incomplete data, shifting hypotheses, multiple scales, and decisions that need to be explained to collaborators.

Research directions

Our research directions meet at a shared question: how can computation make geological evidence more discoverable while respecting its spatial, temporal, and physical context?

We build representations that preserve stratigraphic order, spatial relationships, and uncertainty instead of treating every observation as an isolated label.

From web maps to 3D terrain, our open tools turn complex geoscience workflows into clear, inspectable interfaces for teams.

When an interpretation is ambiguous, our systems surface alternatives and ask for the observation that would distinguish them.

The examples below show how a shared data model lets field geologists, remote-sensing specialists, and software engineers work from the same evidence.

As projects evolve, new measurements and hypotheses can be added without losing the original provenance or the assumptions behind an earlier result.

Reading the Earth system

Geobyte projects turn Earth observations into shared instruments for discovery. We study how models can reveal patterns across scales while remaining grounded in physical processes.

Our current work spans mineral systems, tectonic deformation, climate-sensitive landscapes, and the long record preserved in sedimentary basins.

The practical work matters as much as the model: cleaning data, designing experiments, checking assumptions, and building visual tools that make results easier to discuss.

Reliable geoscience

Geoscience decisions often carry incomplete observations and asymmetric consequences. We therefore treat reliability as a research problem, not a finishing step.

Our evaluations test how models behave when sensors disagree, labels are sparse, or the input falls outside the region used for training.

We compare predictions with independent measurements, document failure modes, and make uncertainty visible alongside every map, classification, and simulation.

A model that cannot explain what it saw, what it assumed, and where it may fail is not ready for the field.

We also test the human workflow around a tool: whether collaborators can reproduce a result, challenge it, and update it when new evidence arrives.

These practices help us build systems that are useful without overstating what the data can support.

The lab publishes protocols and evaluation fixtures alongside code whenever possible, so reliability can be checked rather than simply claimed.

Safety in geoscience also means respecting communities, sensitive locations, and the limits of responsible data sharing.

Open and responsible science

Geobyte practices open and responsible science: we share methods, record decisions, and invite domain experts to challenge our assumptions.

Sensitive datasets require care. We work with data owners and local partners to define appropriate access, attribution, and resolution before a project begins.

We prefer interpretable baselines before complex models, and we keep human review where an automated conclusion could shape a high-stakes decision.

Clear communication is part of the method. We distinguish observation, inference, simulation, and speculation in every public result.

Our software is designed for extension: new sensors, new regions, and new hypotheses should be able to join the system without rewriting its foundations.

We welcome collaborations that make Earth science more reproducible, more legible, and more connected to the communities it serves.

The lab is especially interested in partnerships that join careful field practice with ambitious computational experimentation.

Join the lab

Geobyte welcomes geologists, computer scientists, engineers, and visual storytellers who want to work across disciplines. We value careful questions, generous collaboration, and tools that other researchers can actually use.

We host undergraduate projects, visiting researchers, graduate collaborations, and engineering partnerships around computational geoscience.

To start a conversation, write to hello@geobyte.lab with a short note about the Earth question you want to explore and the perspective you bring.

Open roles and current project briefs are shared with our collaborators first. Tell us what you would build at the boundary of geology and computation.

RESEARCH NOTES

  1. 1

    Geobyte datasets retain measurement provenance, coordinate reference systems, and collection context whenever those details are available.

  2. 2

    Model outputs are reviewed against held-out observations and domain-expert annotations; a high score is never treated as geological proof.

  3. 3

    Remote-sensing examples may combine satellite, UAV, and field observations at different resolutions. The interface keeps those scales explicit.

  4. 4

    Simulation results describe a tested scenario and its assumptions. They are not forecasts without uncertainty and independent validation.

  5. 5

    Open-source releases include a model card, known limitations, and instructions for reproducing the published examples.

  6. 6

    Field partners retain control over sensitive locations and data permissions. Public examples are generalized where necessary.

  7. 7

    The team welcomes corrections, alternative interpretations, and requests for collaboration through hello@geobyte.lab.
